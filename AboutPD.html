Parkinson's Disease

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease where nerve cells, neurons, in the brain are damaged, weakened and eventually die. The most common symptoms from PD appear from neurons located in the substantia nigra at the base of the brain. These neurons produce dopamine which is a chemical messenger that relays signals to the brain to regulate movement and mood. When the neurons get damaged they no longer release dopamine resulting in fewer signals to control movement resulting in symptoms such as tremors, stiffness, impaired balance, walking, talking or overall movement. PD is a progressive disease meaning that the symptoms and intensity worsen over time as more and more neurons die and lose functionality.

It is important to note that PD also impacts the nervous system of the brain. The nerve cells in the brainstem send signals through the nervous system via the neurotransmitter norepinephrine. This chemical messenger controls the automatic functions like blood pressure and pulse.

According to the Parkinson’s Foundation it is estimated that 1.1 million people in the U.S. are living with Parkinson’s disease with roughly 90,000 new diagnoses each year. PD is generally classified into two types; idiopathic or familial. Idiopathic is the most common type and the cause is unknown while familial is passed down through genes. There has been significant research into the causes and treatment of PD however the complete mechanism of the disease is still unknown. However, in recent years scientists have discovered a gene named LRRK2 (pronounced LARK-2) that may have some answers to solving the mystery.
